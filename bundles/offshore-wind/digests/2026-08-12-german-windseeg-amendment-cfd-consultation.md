# What Happened

Germany's Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Energy (BMWE) opened a
consultation on a **draft amendment to the Wind Energy at Sea Act (WindSeeG)**,
presented on **10 August 2026**. The draft reworks the German offshore wind tender
system: it introduces **bilateral Contracts for Difference**, sets **annual tenders
of 2 GW to 4.8 GW**, extends the standard operating period for new wind farms to
**35 years**, opens the way for **cross-border projects**, and retains the target of
**at least 70 GW by 2045**.

The reform follows the **August 2025 tender for the N-10.1 and N-10.2 sites, which
attracted no bids**, after which the government pushed the re-tender of both sites
to **2027**.

The **German Offshore Wind Energy Association (BWO)** welcomed the CfDs but said the
mechanism should be built into the tender design rather than serve as a **"fallback
option"** triggered only when a site finds no developer willing to proceed without
price hedging. BWO also criticised the draft for omitting a mechanism to **return
and re-tender sites awarded in 2023-2025** that have not reached FID — capacity it
argues is otherwise locked up along with its grid connection and sea area.

# Why It Matters

This is the legislative answer to the zero-bid tender that has hung over the German
market for a year, and it moves the largest EU offshore wind market toward the
contracted-revenue model that brought bidders back to Denmark
([Denmark 2.8 GW CfD tender](/tenders/denmark-2-8gw-cfd-tender-2026.md),
[won by Vattenfall on 4 August](/digests/2026-08-04-vattenfall-wins-danish-offshore-wind-tender.md))
and that the [UK CfD scheme](/policy/uk-contracts-for-difference-scheme.md) has run
for a decade. The dispute is now about degree, not direction: whether hedging is the
default or the exception.
