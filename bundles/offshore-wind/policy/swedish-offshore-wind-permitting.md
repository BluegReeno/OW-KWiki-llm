# Overview

Offshore wind farms in Sweden's **exclusive economic zone (EEZ)** are permitted by
decision of the national government, with separate permits required under the
Continental Shelf Act for the subsea export cables that connect a project to the
onshore transmission grid. In practice the binding constraint has not been
environmental impact but **military defence**: the Swedish Armed Forces assess
whether a proposed wind farm conflicts with sea areas designated as being of
national interest for defence, and a negative assessment has consistently led the
government to reject the application.

# Decisions to Date

- **November 2024** — the government approved Vattenfall's **Poseidon** project and
  rejected 13 other applications, citing conflict with defence interests. Earlier
  approvals include **Kattegatt Syd** and **Galene**.
- **24 July 2026** — the government completed decisions on all remaining offshore
  wind permit applications under review, **approving two and rejecting eleven**.
  - Approved: [Fyrskeppet Offshore](/projects/fyrskeppet-offshore.md)
    ([Skyborn Renewables](/companies/skyborn.md)) and
    [Vidar](/projects/vidar.md) ([Vattenfall](/companies/vattenfall.md) and Zephyr).
  - Rejected: Bothnia Offshore Lambda North, Bothnia Offshore Sigma, Eystrasalt
    Offshore, Gretas Klackar 1, Gävle Öst Havsvindpark, Mareld, Najaderna, Olof
    Skötkonung, Polargrund Offshore, Sylen, and Västvind.

For the rejected projects the government held that, despite their contribution to
fossil-free electricity generation and the mitigation measures proposed, the
importance of the areas for defence interests did not allow them to proceed. The
associated Continental Shelf Act cable permit requests were dismissed without
further examination.

# Conditions on Approvals

Both projects approved in July 2026 must be **completed within ten years, by 2036**.
Before construction can begin, their developers must still obtain the separate
permits for the subsea export cables needed to reach the Swedish onshore grid.

# Shift Toward an Auction-Based System

The government stated that the refusals do **not** permanently exclude the affected
sea areas from offshore wind development: they could be reconsidered once Sweden
transitions to an **auction-based system** for offshore wind. This makes the
defence screening a de facto gatekeeper for which areas can eventually be put to
tender, rather than an outright and permanent exclusion.

# Market Consequences

Permitting is not the only hurdle. Shortly after Vidar was approved, Vattenfall's
CEO Anna Borg was reported by Reuters as saying the company would not proceed with
the project at this time because current market conditions do not make it
commercially viable — an approved-but-shelved outcome that underlines the absence,
so far, of a Swedish offshore wind support/auction mechanism.
